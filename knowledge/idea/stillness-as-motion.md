# Stillness as motion

## Question

How can holding still make motion stronger than moving all the time?

## Then

Japanese television animation, drawing fewer frames out of necessity, discovered that
contrast in time is more powerful than smoothness. A character holds a pose (*tome*), the
tension builds in the hold (*tame*), then the action happens in very few frames and lands on
a strong pose that is held again. Animators known for effects drew explosions and impacts
as bold, angular shapes with smears and flashes on single frames. Others choreographed
swarms of missiles on spiraling paths with extreme speed changes. Drawings updated every 2 or
3 frames instead of every frame give a rhythm that smooth interpolation lacks. Western
animation calls the same family of ideas anticipation, timing and spacing, but the Japanese
practice pushed the held frame into an expression of its own.

## On screen

- Long hold, short action, long hold. The action takes a fraction of the time of the holds around it.
- Before the action: a slow push or a tremble during the hold (tension), then a jump cut to the move.
- The action itself on 1s or 2s with smears, speed lines or a single-frame flash at the hit.
- After the hit: a held impact pose, often with a camera shake that decays.
- Stepped time: values update 8 to 12 times per second, not every frame.

Anchors: Yoshinori Kanada's effects animation; Ichiro Itano's missile sequences (the "Itano circus").

## Now

Interpolation is free and smooth: every tool tweens every frame, and generated video is
smooth by default. Smoothness has become the sign of the automatic. Scarce: timing with
intent; the held frame; motion that hits.

## Openings

- Stepped timing on purpose for a "drawn" feel in otherwise digital work; let one element run
  smooth so the contrast is visible.
- A beat drop as a single-frame impact after a long tame: the breath before the peak, extended.
- In a feed of continuous motion, a piece that is mostly still, with three hits.
