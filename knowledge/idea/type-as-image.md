# Type as image

## Question

What if letters are seen before they are read: their weight, size, breaks and direction
carry the meaning before the words do?

## Then

Avant-garde poets and poster designers broke lines across the page so that layout itself
spoke. In motion, this became title cards where the typography is the shot. A landmark
Japanese anime used huge, extra-bold condensed Mincho (serif) type, white on black, with
lines broken at odd places and stacked into L-shapes, held for seconds or flashed for a few
frames. The look traced back to a film director's title cards decades earlier. Later
anime cut in single-frame cards of dense red-and-black text, too fast to read in real time:
written for fans who pause, rewind and screenshot.

## On screen

- One typeface pushed to an extreme: heaviest weight, tightest setting, largest size.
- Line breaks chosen for shape, not grammar; words cropped by the frame.
- Japanese: vertical and horizontal writing in one frame; Mincho for gravity, gothic for noise;
  a single kanji at full frame height as an image.
- Duration as meaning: a card held in silence is a statement; a 2-frame card is a subliminal hit.
- Palette of two values (white on black) plus one color at most.

Anchors: *Neon Genesis Evangelion* (1995) title cards in Matisse EB. Shaft's text-card
editing in *Bakemonogatari* (2009).

## Now

Auto-captions put text on every video, in the same neutral style. Variable fonts and
kinetic type presets make animated text easy. Scarce: letterforms with a voice; layouts that
only work in one language; frames designed to be paused, since viewers screenshot and share
single frames.

## Openings

- Pause-bait: frames that reward stopping, with text that is unreadable in motion and
  complete when still. The loop invites the pause.
- A variable-font axis (weight, width) as an emotional dial tied to the voice or the story.
- Two writing systems in one composition, where the direction change is the turn of the story.
