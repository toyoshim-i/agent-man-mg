# Identity as system

## Question

How can something stay recognizable while it never looks the same twice?

## Then

A traditional logo is one fixed drawing, reproduced exactly. Early music television kept
only the outline of its letterform fixed and filled it with a different pattern, texture or
animation every time, made by many artists. The logo became a container. A new television
channel in Britain built its ident from colored blocks that flew together in 3D space and
assembled into the channel's number: identity as an event of assembly, not a picture. Later
identities were defined as rules (a grid, a color logic, a motion behavior) that designers or
software applied to every case, so variety itself became recognizable.

## On screen

- A fixed part (outline, grid, proportion, motion behavior) and a variable part (fill, color,
  content, data).
- The mark is often the end state of a process: pieces assembling, a pattern resolving.
- Every instance differs, and the set reads as one family.
- Motion behavior can be the identity: a particular easing, rhythm or direction.

Anchors: MTV logo (Manhattan Design, 1981). Channel 4 ident (Martin Lambie-Nairn, 1982).

## Now

Every logo can be animated by a template, and responsive logos adapt to every size. So
motion and variation alone no longer distinguish anyone. Scarce: a rule that states the
organization's principle; identity recognizable from motion alone, with the mark hidden.

## Openings

- Motion signature: a brand recognizable by how things move (its timing curve, its rhythm),
  even when the logo is absent.
- The mark as the result of the brand's principle: a precise company's mark is order emerging
  from disorder; a community's mark is assembled from contributions.
- Context-driven fill: time of day, place or live data changes the variable part while the
  fixed part holds.
