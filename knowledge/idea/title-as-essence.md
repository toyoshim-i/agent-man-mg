# Title as essence

## Question

How can an opening compress the whole work into a single symbol, so the audience knows what
it is *about* before anything happens?

## Then

Mid-century film credits were static cards over curtains; audiences talked through them.
Graphic designers proposed that the title should set the mood and state the film's core in
symbolic terms. A cut-paper arm, jagged and broken, stood for addiction. Spirals stood for
obsession. Horizontal bars slid across the frame and split the names apart, for a story about
a split mind. Decades later, an opening built from a killer's handmade notebooks, scratched
film and jittering type put the audience inside the antagonist's mind, and revived the form.
Later television openings layered faces and landscapes in double exposure to state a theme.

## On screen

- One symbol taken from the core of the story, abstracted to flat shapes or one texture.
- Credits obey the symbol's grammar: if the symbol splits, the names split.
- No scenes from the work. The opening is a statement, not a trailer.
- The title resolves last, as the symbol's conclusion.

Anchors: Saul Bass, *The Man with the Golden Arm* (1955), *Vertigo* (1958, with John Whitney),
*Psycho* (1960). Kyle Cooper, *Se7en* (1995).

## Now

Streaming offers "skip intro", so long openings are skipped. Brand stings and template logo
reveals are everywhere and say nothing about the brand. Scarce: a symbol that compresses;
an opening short enough to survive the feed and dense enough to be worth it.

## Openings

- One-second essence: in a vertical feed, the symbol must land in the first second, then the
  rest of the piece unpacks it.
- A logo sting as the title of a company: not "the logo appears", but "what this company is
  about", stated by the motion that produces the mark.
- A symbol that recurs across a series of short pieces, so the series is the opening.
