# Rule as work

## Question

What if the artwork is the rule, and each image is just one outcome of it?

## Then

Conceptual artists wrote wall drawings as instructions that others carried out, so the work
was the instruction and every installation was a performance of it. Early computer artists
used plotters to draw systematic variations of squares and lines, introducing a small amount
of disorder into strict order, and found that a little randomness makes order feel alive.
A programming environment made for artists and designers opened generative work to a whole
generation. Later, platforms published algorithms whose outputs were minted one by one, so
collectors owned an outcome of a rule they could not see in advance.

## On screen

- A family of outputs that share a structure and differ in detail.
- Order with a measured amount of disorder: a grid with a few percent of elements displaced,
  rotated or missing.
- Variations shown together (a grid of outcomes) so the rule becomes visible through comparison.
- Process can be visible: the image builds itself in front of the viewer.

Anchors: Sol LeWitt's wall drawings. Vera Molnar's plotter works. Processing (Casey Reas and
Ben Fry, 2001).

## Now

Generative presets and generated images make endless variations free. Variation alone means
nothing. Scarce: a rule with meaning; curation (which outcomes are worth showing); a rule
that the viewer can understand and take part in.

## Openings

- The viewer is a parameter: a rule whose inputs come from the audience (their sound, their
  movement, their phone), so each person sees their own outcome.
- One rule, one brand: a single generative rule produces every asset, and the rule is published
  as part of the identity.
- Show the disorder parameter: a piece that slowly turns randomness up from 0% to 100%, and the
  meaning is where order breaks.
