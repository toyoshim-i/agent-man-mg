# In-camera truth

## Question

How can an image prove that it really happened?

## Then

By the late 1990s computer graphics could show anything, and the impossible stopped being
amazing. Music video makers answered with things that were hard because they were real:
tricks done in the camera with mirrors, loops and precise timing; whole videos shot in one
continuous take of choreography on moving treadmills; chain-reaction machines built across
a warehouse; videos assembled from hundreds of fans' webcams. The thrill was knowing it was
real, and the evidence was in the image: no cut to hide behind, visible effort, small mistakes.

## On screen

- One continuous take, or a structure that makes cheating impossible to hide.
- Physical materials doing the work: paper, light, water, bodies, objects.
- Visible effort and small imperfections kept in.
- The trick is revealed or guessable, and the viewer enjoys solving it.

Anchors: Michel Gondry's in-camera music videos. OK Go, "Here It Goes Again" (2006).
Sour, "Hibi no Neiro" (2009, made from fans' webcams).

## Now

Generated video can fake anything, including "one takes" and "handmade" textures. Real and
fake look alike, so provenance has become the scarce thing: proof of process, liveness,
and the trace of specific people. A handmade mark reads as authorship.

## Openings

- The making is part of the piece: the process (sketches, takes, code being typed) is shown
  inside the work as evidence.
- Physical sources for digital work: scan real ink drops, paper fibers, light leaks, and build
  the motion from them; say so.
- Live proof: a piece that responds to something only the real moment can provide (the
  audience's sound, the actual time), so it could not have been pre-made.
